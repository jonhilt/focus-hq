# Briefings

A plugin (`FOCUS_PLUGINS=briefings`, [plugins](../../docs/plugins.md)), switched on in each project's settings too (the manager's **Projects** tab, under **Plugins**): a goal's lead can walk the captain through a concept with an animated explainer on their station's screen, narrated in their voice up close, and answer questions about the beat the captain's on. Without it, a lead's office has no `explain` tool, and the screen shows what they put up with show, model and ask as it always does; explainers already on a screen still play (they're self-contained pages), with no Voice button and no Ask box.

## The explainer

for a concept rather than a code path, when you need to understand how something works (a mechanism, what happens when it fails, how data moves, why one design beats another), the lead walks you through it with an animated explainer rather than paragraphs (`mcp__office__explain`). They give its beats, each a caption and how long it plays, and a stage that draws the whole scene from the time t; the office wraps them in the ship's LCARS player (`explainerPage` in `explainer.ts`): an elbow frame, the beats as numbered blocks down the left, the caption under the stage, and ◀ Step, Play and Step ▶ with a timeline along the bottom. It goes up like any of show's visuals, sharing their ids. Until you press **Run briefing** it rests, dimmed, on its last frame, so the screen's picture sums it up. The briefing room's screen shows that picture, like any other visual's, and redraws only when something changes. Up close it's the live page: Play runs it through, Step (or ← and →) plays one beat and pauses (its caption staying up over its last frame), and clicking a beat's block or its part of the timeline jumps there. Up close, **Voice** (along the bottom, off until you switch it on, and remembered by the browser) has the lead talk you through it: each beat has a narration they wrote for it (or, if they left one out, its title and caption read out), said in the lead's own voice by Kokoro on this machine (see [Number One's voice mode](../number-one/README.md#voice-mode), whose voice kit it shares), while the beat plays. The voice sets the pace: a beat holds on its last frame until its line's finished, then the next one plays. Pause pauses the voice too and Play picks it up; Step, a beat's block or the timeline cut it short and start afresh. The page runs sandboxed, so it can't reach the office itself: it posts each line to the screen panel around it, where the plugin's part of the screen hears it (`narrator.ts`, put round the page through the core's `screen` hook in `client.ts`), which fetches the first beats' audio as soon as the voice is on, and each next beat's as one plays: only a beat ahead, since Kokoro says one line at a time for the whole office (the core's `server/speech.ts`), and lines wanted now (an answer, voice mode) go ahead of lines fetched ahead of time (`nextLine`). A line nobody's waiting for any more (hushed, or its page closed) is dropped from the queue. A beat whose line isn't ready yet (Kokoro still loading, say) holds until it is: the browser's own voice says it only if Kokoro has failed, and then the best of the system's voices (a premium or neural one if there is, never a novelty or robotic one; `crewVoice.ts`). Once Kokoro's downloaded, the office loads it as it starts (not in the demo office), so the first line is quick too. Under anything the lead has shown, while their goal's open, is an **Ask** box (`ask.ts`): type a question and press Enter, or press **Mic** and say it, and it's sent as soon as it's transcribed. Asking pauses the briefing (the player takes a `pause` message, and tells the screen which beat it's on as that changes), and the lead hears the question with the briefing's id and the beat you were on, and that their answer is read aloud as they write it, so they keep it short and spoken (`briefingQuestion` in `shared.ts`). What they write back while answering (a line before they look something up, then the answer) shows under the briefing and is said in their voice: always if you asked aloud, and if you typed it, when the briefing's Voice is on. Beside it is how long their first reply took. That's when the lead's between turns. While they're in the middle of something, a question would wait behind their work, so the **quick answerer** takes it instead (`whoAnswers` in `quickAnswer.ts`, routed by `desk.ts`, behind `POST /api/plugins/briefings/ask`): Haiku, given the briefing beat by beat (the captain's beat marked), the goal's note and what the lead said lately, and able to read the lead's workspace, answers in a few spoken sentences. Its answer comes straight back, labelled as the quick answer and said in a voice that's never the lead's (`quickVoice`), and the lead hears the question and the answer (from the Quick answerer) to put it right if it's wrong. If it can't answer, the question goes to the lead after all. Answers are said a sentence at a time (`sentences` in `shared/voice.ts`), so the first plays while Kokoro makes the rest. If they explain again under the same id (asked for more on a part, say), the new version replaces the briefing without cutting off the answer, and says what changed: the screen compares its beats' lines and titles with the version before (`firstChangedBeat` in `shared.ts`), puts **Updated · beat N changed** by its title, and the player waits at the start of that beat (a `show` message), to play from there rather than from the top. Only the latest question's answer is shown or said: one asked over, or the briefing closed, is dropped as it arrives. `pnpm run voice:check` times the whole dialog, from a spoken question to the answer heard, against its budgets ([scripts](../../docs/scripts.md)). The player runs the stage through every moment as it loads, so a stage that throws puts a Stage fault panel up, and the lead hears about it: the office passes on whatever errors a page reports while it takes the picture, for show's visuals too (`pageErrorsNote` in the core's `lead.ts`). A stage can be 3D: with `libs: ["three"]` (and any three.js addons by path) the player loads the office's own three.js from a CDN through an import map before the lead's script runs (`KIT_LOADER`), and puts it on `window.THREE`; the lead's `render(t)` sets the camera and every object from t and draws the WebGL frame itself. The page says when it has drawn its resting frame (`kitReady`), and the office waits for that before taking the picture, so a stage that loads slowly isn't caught blank. A 3D stage starts from `kit3d()` (`KIT_3D`): the renderer behind the stage's HTML, studio lighting, a camera and a grid, named objects, HTML labels pinned to objects (a label shows only on frames whose render(t) places it, so jumping back to before it appears hides it), and camera shots, one per beat, that hold still through the beat and move only at its start; drawing leaves `window.__scene` saying where the camera, objects and labels ended up. Every explainer (3D or not) gets looked at for its lead: in the same visit as the screen's picture, the office jumps to the middle of each beat (`kitSeek`) and takes a picture of the stage (`LeadToolContext.showFrames`), the player says what it sees in it (`kitSeen`: how flat it is, where the labels landed), and the tool checks that (`momentNotes`): a stage that's one flat colour, and kit3d labels out of view, cut off or overlapping. The explain tool's reply carries those pictures and what the checks found, so the lead sees their explainer as the captain will and fixes it before you open it. The research behind it, on what makes agents' 3D animations work, is in [3d-explainers](../../docs/3d-explainers.md).

## Where it lives

`plugins/briefings/`: `server.ts` (the plugin: its lead tool, and the `POST /api/plugins/briefings/ask` route), `explainTool.ts` (the `explain` tool: its input, its description and what it answers), `explainer.ts` (the LCARS player, pure), `desk.ts` (where a question goes), `quickAnswer.ts` (the quick answerer: its prompt, and Haiku's session or the demo's stand-in), `demo.ts` (the explainer the demo's scripted leads put up), `shared.ts`, `client.ts` (its part of the station's screen up close), `narrator.ts`, `ask.ts`, `voice.ts` (whether the voice is on, per browser) and `briefings.css`, and `scripts/` (`voice:check`).
